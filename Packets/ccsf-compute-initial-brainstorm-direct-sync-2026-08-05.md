### Updated thesis

The concept is not simply a City-owned data center. It is a **governed municipal AI service** whose compute, model routing, data controls, worker participation, energy behavior, and public accountability are designed together.

The central proposition for a public post:

> Use a measured, worker-designed pilot to test whether public AI can improve services without surrendering sensitive data or locking the City to one model company. Scale only if the evidence supports a regional, labor-friendly public compute service that is cost-effective, behaves as a flexible grid asset, and helps California make fuller use of clean energy — ultimately including offshore wind delivered reliably to the Bay Area.

Public ownership is not self-justifying. The concept earns the right to scale only if it demonstrates useful public services, protects workers and residents, avoids shifting costs to ratepayers, and performs better than the available subscription, cloud, and colocation alternatives.

### The same principles at pilot and infrastructure scale

| Principle | Pilot scale: 100–200 workers | Larger scale: CCSF and a regional public consortium |
|---|---|---|
| **Public purpose** | Test a few bounded, high-value workflows with named human owners and affected-worker input. | Operate a governed public AI service for demonstrated municipal needs, not speculative demand. |
| **Cost effectiveness** | Compare total workflow cost, quality, and staff time against existing work and proprietary services. Avoid a capital build. | Compare lifecycle cost and utilization across subscription, cloud, colocation, CCSF-owned, and consortium options before procurement. |
| **Model independence** | Use a capability-tier gateway, portable retrieval and evaluation assets, open-weight models where they perform adequately, and a frontier-model escape hatch. | Keep interfaces, data, evaluations, and workflow definitions portable across model and hardware generations; procure capabilities rather than named models. |
| **Data security** | Enforce data classification at the gateway. Do not admit sensitive workflows until identity, logging, isolation, retrieval, and incident controls pass. | Keep municipal data, keys, access policy, and audit evidence under public control even if some physical capacity is colocated or contracted. |
| **Labor** | Co-design workflows with workers and labor representatives; measure job quality, workload, training, service quality, and error burden rather than assuming head-count savings. | Use prevailing-wage and union labor, apprenticeship and training pathways, project-labor and community-benefit agreements where appropriate, and durable public technical jobs. |
| **Energy and water** | Prefer an existing facility with credible energy, water, and labor disclosure. Measure energy and carbon per useful workflow and test whether safe batch work can move in time. | Make dedicated infrastructure regenerative: clean supply, flexible load, storage and grid services, low- or no-water-consumption cooling where feasible, no harmful discharge, strict noise limits, and useful heat recovery where there is a real nearby load. |
| **Accountability** | Publish the evaluation method, benefits, failures, energy use, security events, and go/no-go criteria at an appropriate level of detail. | Maintain a public dashboard covering service value, reliability, cost, energy, emissions, water, workforce commitments, and promised versus realized community benefits. |
| **Stranded-asset protection** | Use short, modular commitments and retain an exit path. | Stage capacity against measured demand, design for multiple model and accelerator generations, and use multiple sites or partners where that materially improves resilience. |

The pilot does **not** need a purpose-built green data center to express the regenerative idea. At pilot scale, regeneration means proving the governance and software behaviors that a later facility would need: workload measurement, carbon- and grid-aware scheduling, transparency, ratepayer protection, worker participation, portable models and workflows, and a hard stop if public value is not demonstrated.

### Regenerative data center: the updated environmental and political standard

The June 2026 Jigar Shah–Gary Dorris–Kerry Duggan–James Connaughton op-ed supplied the term **regenerative data center**. Shah's April 15 Volts conversation adds the system logic: new load can support lower-cost grid investment only when the customer helps unlock existing capacity, accepts flexible interconnection where appropriate, adds storage or other capacity, and does not transfer its costs and risks to ordinary ratepayers.

For this concept, regenerative should be a testable design and operating standard, not a branding claim:

- **Grid-positive:** participate in demand response and other grid services; buffer volatile compute loads; shift deferrable workloads toward periods of abundant clean generation; bring storage or pay for the capacity needed to serve the load.
- **Ratepayer-safe:** no subsidy or cost-allocation structure that leaves households financing speculative compute capacity or dedicated upgrades without commensurate public benefit.
- **Low-impact:** eliminate direct fossil generation as the normal supply strategy; minimize water consumption and discharge; set enforceable noise, air-quality, and land-use limits; account for embodied carbon and equipment turnover.
- **Labor- and community-positive:** engage workers, labor, host communities, tribes, and other affected groups before siting and design choices harden; make jobs, training, procurement, and community benefits enforceable rather than promotional.
- **Transparent:** publicly report operating performance against the commitments above.

The Volts argument is especially useful because it reframes flexibility as infrastructure rather than sacrifice. Batteries, transmission upgrades, grid-enhancing technology, distributed capacity, and controllable load can remain useful even if forecast AI demand fails to materialize. That no-regrets standard belongs in every public-compute capital decision.

### Jeremy's long-term energy vision: maximize offshore wind with redundant underwater transmission

**Human direction recorded 2026-08-05:** At mature regional or state scale, the desired energy end state is to maximize California offshore wind and deliver it to the Bay Area through redundant, physically diverse underwater transmission paths. Public compute should be a flexible, grid-supporting user of that system — able to move deferrable workloads toward high-wind periods and support storage economics — rather than a conventional inflexible load.

This vision has a direct Humboldt research antecedent. The Schatz Energy Research Center at Cal Poly Humboldt has studied North Coast offshore wind since 2019 across generation, transmission, ratepayer impacts, workforce, ports, permitting, environmental conditions, and community priorities. Its **2020 Humboldt–San Francisco Bay Subsea Transmission Cable Conceptual Assessment** evaluated an approximately 250-mile HVDC link and developed nearshore and offshore corridor concepts. It concluded at the planning level that one cable bundle could carry roughly 1.8 GW and that installing two cables along different routes could provide redundancy if one were damaged. It also identified unusually hard constraints — subsea canyons and landslides, seismic faults, sandwaves outside the Golden Gate, deep-water technology limits, fishing interactions, habitat, and existing telecommunications cables. That work makes the redundant-subsea vision a research-backed option, not a blank-sheet idea, while underscoring that it remains pre-feasibility rather than an engineered route.

Schatz is therefore the most relevant locally grounded research source for the next transmission pass. Its work should be reviewed alongside CAISO and California Energy Commission planning before the vision is refined. Any direct outreach or proposed partnership remains a separate human decision; this note does not authorize engagement.

This is a long-term infrastructure vision, not a claim about the current CCSF concept or an approved project. It has three distinct layers:

1. **Generate:** develop California's offshore wind resource responsibly and at enough scale to complement daytime solar, including the North Coast resource.
2. **Deliver resiliently:** develop multiple transmission paths with separate cable routes, landing points, and grid interconnections where engineering, cost, marine, tribal, fisheries, and community review supports them. The objective is useful transmission headroom and resilience to a cable, landing, converter, or inland-grid outage — not redundancy as a slogan.
3. **Use intelligently:** combine regional storage, flexible interconnection, and controllable demand. Public compute can shift batch inference, indexing, evaluation, and other delay-tolerant work toward periods of abundant wind while keeping public-service workloads within their latency and reliability requirements.

The current baseline is narrower. California's adopted offshore-wind planning goal is up to **25 GW by 2045**, with workforce, union-labor, tribal, environmental, port, and transmission requirements integral to the plan. CAISO's approved 2023–2024 transmission plan enables an initial **1.6 GW** of Humboldt-area wind through a new Humboldt substation, an approximately **260-mile Humboldt–Collinsville line planned for eventual HVDC operation but initially operated as 500 kV AC**, and a separate approximately **140-mile 500 kV AC line to Fern Road**, with a planning in-service year of 2034. Those are two delivery paths, but they are **not** the envisioned pair of redundant underwater HVDC routes into the Bay Area; final routing and permitting also remain future work.

Three later Schatz findings sharpen the long-term framing. Its May 2026 ratepayer analysis estimates that recovering the cost of the two CAISO-approved projects across the CAISO customer base over their expected 50-year life would average about **$0.28/MWh, or $1.68 per year for an average California household**, in 2025 dollars, peaking at an estimated $4.52 per household in 2035. This is encouraging evidence for the ratepayer-safe principle, but it covers the approved substation and two lines under stated cost-recovery assumptions — not a second separated subsea HVDC cable or the full offshore-wind vision. Separately, Schatz's Northern California and Southern Oregon study evaluates regional onshore and undersea transmission scenarios connecting **7.2–25.8 GW** of potential generation. Its central planning lesson is directly applicable here: early projects should be designed against a long-term regional topology so they do not create avoidable impacts, bottlenecks, or stranded investment.

Its 2025 workforce analysis also makes the labor thesis more precise. Job totals depend heavily on how much of the supply chain stays in California; in its modeled 1.5 GW Humboldt project, component manufacturing accounts for about 60% of jobs, while operations and maintenance account for less than 18%. The public argument therefore should not promise that the data center itself will be a large permanent-job engine. The defensible labor case spans union construction, transmission and port work, in-state manufacturing, apprenticeships, worker-led AI implementation, and durable public operations roles.

The strategic relationship must stay proportionate. Municipal compute demand is tiny beside a gigawatt-scale wind and transmission buildout. CCSF compute would neither anchor nor justify it. The stronger public-interest formulation is that offshore transmission should serve statewide decarbonization, electrification, affordability, and reliability, while public compute demonstrates how a flexible municipal load can help the broader system use clean generation more effectively.

Questions for the next offshore-wind research pass:

- How should Schatz's 2020 nearshore and offshore Humboldt–SF Bay corridor concepts be updated against the CAISO-approved topology, current cable technology, seabed data, and the larger North Coast wind scenarios it has studied since?
- What second subsea HVDC corridor and Bay Area interconnection could create genuine physical and electrical diversity from the planned Humboldt–Collinsville path?
- How would a redundant subsea path change the ratepayer allocation, system benefits, congestion savings, and household impacts in Schatz's 2026 analysis?
- Should later North Coast lease development share cable corridors to reduce environmental disturbance, or use distinct corridors to reduce common-mode failure risk?
- Where should storage sit — offshore-wind interconnection, Bay Area load center, municipal facility, or a portfolio across all three — to reduce curtailment and congestion most effectively?
- What transmission, port, manufacturing, project-labor, community-benefit, and tribal agreements would ensure the larger buildout is labor-friendly and publicly legitimate?
- Which compute workloads are genuinely shiftable, for how long, and what is their value relative to other flexible loads? Pilot telemetry should answer this before the energy vision is used in sizing.

### Recovered public-post spine

1. **The conflict:** data-center growth is becoming a fight over utility bills, water, land, emissions, jobs, and who receives the benefit.
2. **The municipal need:** CCSF still needs useful, secure AI and cannot prudently make every workflow dependent on one proprietary model provider.
3. **The pilot:** test 100–200 workers and a small set of governed workflows before buying infrastructure.
4. **The architecture:** open-weight models where capable, frontier APIs where necessary and legally eligible, with portable routing, data controls, and evaluations preventing model lock-in.
5. **The labor compact:** design with workers, invest in skills and durable public technical capacity, and evaluate work quality rather than leading with staff reduction.
6. **The regenerative standard:** turn compute into a flexible, transparent grid participant with enforceable energy, water, noise, ratepayer, and community protections.
7. **The scale path:** if the evidence clears the gate, aggregate demand, expertise, resilience, and procurement through a public consortium while resolving pooled versus federated infrastructure deliberately.
8. **The larger horizon:** use storage, flexible demand, and ultimately redundant subsea transmission to maximize the public value of California offshore wind for the Bay Area — with compute as one small, controllable beneficiary.
9. **The condition:** no dedicated facility or major capital commitment until measured demand and a credible public-benefit case justify it.
