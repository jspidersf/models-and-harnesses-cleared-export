## Purpose and context

This packet transfers the public-source research on carbon, electricity-grid behavior, utility cost allocation, and host-community impacts that should inform any future CCSF public-compute concept. It is separate from the already-published initial-brainstorm packet and does not repeat or resolve that packet's work-side disposition.

The central finding is that public ownership changes who receives the benefit, but does not remove infrastructure burdens or the need for evidence. Public legitimacy should be a decision gate before dedicated infrastructure, not a communications layer added afterward.

This is public-source personal research. It is not evidence that CCSF has authorized a project, that a department sponsored the concept, or that any pilot, site, utility arrangement, procurement, external engagement, or capital decision is approved.

## Research scope and method

The review combined California Energy Commission forecasting methodology, the CAISO large-load initiative and technical straw proposal, public CCSF comments in that proceeding, NERC large-load reliability material, California legislation, Berkeley Lab and Virginia reviews, peer-reviewed community-impact research, national polling, and public political-economy reporting.

The method separated planning baselines from operational capability, adopted policy from proposals, City public filings from project authorization, public attitudes from lived experience and causal effects, and regional averages from concentrated local burdens.

## Findings

### 1. Renewable supply is not sufficient; actual load behavior must be measured

**Public-source fact:** the California Energy Commission's reviewed 2025 forecast methodology found sampled existing data centers averaged roughly 85–90% of annual maximum demand across hours, with little day/night variation.

**Analysis, high confidence:** labels such as “batch,” “deferrable,” or “carbon-aware” do not reduce utility capacity assumptions. A pilot must measure the quantity of load that can move, shift duration, notice required, recovery/ramp behavior, service impact, and actual cost. Dedicated-site studies and contracts should translate those measurements into assumptions, telemetry, commissioning tests, curtailment controls, and enforceable terms.

**Caveat:** the CEC sample is a planning baseline for existing data centers, not a prediction that governed public compute cannot be more flexible.

### 2. Computational loads create dynamic grid obligations beyond annual energy totals

**Public-source fact:** NERC's 2026 large-load work called for load modeling, stability studies, commissioning, dynamic recording, controls, and direct operational communication for large computational loads. The CAISO June 2026 technical straw similarly explored requirements for transmission-connected computational loads.

**Recommendation:** measure peak/transient load behavior, ramp and drop rates, oscillation, curtailment performance, recovery, and buffering/UPS/BESS response. At colocation scale, allocate these responsibilities in the facility/operator contract. At a dedicated large site, the owner or governing entity would require direct accountability.

**Caveat:** the CAISO straw was not adopted as of the review date, did not define the final large-load threshold, and may not directly govern a distribution-connected site.

### 3. Carbon accounting must connect infrastructure use to accepted public-service outcomes

**Recommendation, high confidence:** report kWh, carbon intensity, water, peak demand, curtailment, utilization, and useful work per energy unit together with total cost per accepted workflow, cloud/API counterfactual, processing-time or backlog change, quality/error rate, review burden, and workforce participation. Infrastructure efficiency without service outcomes does not establish public value.

Carbon-aware scheduling may be valuable, but it must remain inside service, records, deadline, authorization, and reliability constraints. Renewable procurement claims should distinguish contractual supply, hourly load matching, additionality, local grid effects, and actual marginal emissions.

### 4. Utility cost allocation and stranded-asset protection are core design questions

California SB 57 authorizes the California Public Utilities Commission to assess data-center procurement, transmission, distribution, and stranded-asset cost shifts and identify mitigation opportunities by 2027. That process will not answer every municipal-utility or joint-governance question, but it is a key input for any investor-owned-utility-served colocation or dedicated load.

**Recommendation:** for every siting scenario, identify who supplies generation, distribution, interconnection, capacity upgrades, and backup power; who pays deposits and dedicated-facility costs; who bears underutilization or exit risk; and how unrelated residential or municipal customers are protected from material cost shifts.

Do not claim that California data-center load necessarily raises or lowers other customers' bills before the applicable service, study, and cost path is established.

### 5. The public CCSF CAISO filing supplies principles, not project authority

**Public-source fact:** CCSF's February 25, 2026 CAISO filing supported municipal and non-participating transmission-owner participation, system-wide review of network upgrades, carefully designed non-firm service, comparable treatment of wholesale and retail loads with similar physical impacts, and demand-linked transmission cost recovery.

**Analysis:** these principles are directly relevant to a future burden-allocation gate. The filing is evidence of a public City position in the proceeding. It is not evidence that any department sponsored this concept or that CCSF authorized a compute project.

### 6. Public ownership reduces private-extraction concerns but does not eliminate legitimacy risk

National polling and reporting show significant opposition to nearby data centers, but poll wording and national aggregates do not establish site-specific conditions. A public-service concept differs from hyperscaler projects in ownership, scale, and beneficiaries. It still must prove useful outcomes, avoid stranded public capital, govern workforce effects, protect ratepayers, and give host communities enforceable influence over burdens.

**Recommendation:** use a public-benefit and burden-allocation gate before a dedicated facility or major power/water investment. The decision package should include:

- measured service outcomes and total workflow economics from a bounded pilot;
- actual utility, distribution, colocation, and power-procurement relationships;
- protections against material cost shifts and stranded dedicated assets;
- enforceable energy, water, noise, air, emissions, and backup-generation limits;
- grid-compatible load behavior, telemetry, commissioning, curtailment, and appropriately sized buffering;
- early labor and host-community engagement;
- independent pre-construction baseline, ongoing monitoring, public disaggregated results, and a grievance/remedy process; and
- reporting that compares promised benefits and burdens with realized performance.

### 7. Community evidence needs three distinct streams

**Recommendation informed by public studies:** keep public attitudes, reported lived experience, and causal physical outcomes separate.

- Polls measure attitudes and political risk; they do not measure local exposure.
- Reported experience should be documented even when causation remains uncertain.
- Physical mechanisms should be tested using measurements suited to noise, air, water, traffic, construction, grid, or other exposures.

Do not use a regional average to erase concentrated harm. For a dedicated site, establish pre-construction conditions; separate construction from operations; compare distance bands and relevant control areas where feasible; disaggregate benefits and burdens by proximity and vulnerability; and evaluate whether affected people had meaningful influence and whether mitigation prevented or remedied harm.

“Data centers destroy communities” is too general to serve as either a finding or a dismissal target. Site-specific evidence must carry the decision.

### 8. Pilot-before-capital remains the sequencing rule

**Recommendation:** a bounded workload pilot should precede confident infrastructure sizing. It should measure service outcomes, acceptance criteria, review burden, labor participation, total cost, hosted counterfactual, tokens/compute, memory/cache pressure, peak/transient load behavior, rollback, failure recovery, authority boundaries, records, and named human ownership.

No capital inference should rest on nominal user counts, model token rates, renewable labels, or vendor benchmark efficiency without measured workflow and load traces.

## Technical detail

Minimum infrastructure-impact telemetry for a pilot or site study:

- accepted workflow count and service outcome;
- human review/rework, failure, retry, and fallback paths;
- input/output tokens, context/cache growth, concurrency, queueing, and accelerator utilization;
- IT energy and whole-facility energy, carbon intensity, water, and cooling mode;
- p50/p95 load, ramp/drop rate, oscillation, curtailment quantity/duration/notice, and recovery;
- power source, distribution/interconnection path, dedicated upgrades, backup generation, UPS/BESS behavior, and cost responsibility;
- construction and operational noise, air, water, traffic, and other site-specific exposures;
- baseline and ongoing measurements by distance and relevant vulnerability dimensions; and
- promised versus realized benefits, burdens, mitigation, grievances, and remedies.

The evidence gate should specify who owns each measurement, which source is authoritative, reporting cadence, public disclosure level, thresholds, corrective action, and stop/exit behavior.

## Alternatives and conflicting evidence

- Public ownership can align benefits with residents and agencies, but it does not eliminate local environmental or fiscal burdens.
- Renewable contracts can reduce attributed annual emissions while leaving hourly matching, additionality, transmission, and local reliability unresolved.
- Flexible computation could supply grid value, but existing data-center load shapes show that flexibility must be demonstrated, not assumed.
- National polls indicate political risk but differ by wording and cannot replace site-specific engagement or measurements.
- Regional economic or environmental averages can show broad trends but may conceal concentrated neighborhood harm.
- Colocation can transfer some facility obligations to an operator; it does not remove the need for contractual evidence and cost allocation.

## Caveats and unresolved questions

- The applicable CAISO/utility path depends on site, voltage, distribution/transmission connection, load size, and future adopted rules.
- SB 57 and related proceedings were incomplete at the review date.
- No site-specific baseline, utility study, environmental review, workload trace, or host-community process is included.
- Survey results and external case studies do not predict San Francisco, Sunol, Sierra, or other host-community outcomes.
- Carbon, water, ratepayer, and community effects depend on scale and site; no capital sizing or siting decision follows.
- The concept remains personal public-source research without project authorization.
